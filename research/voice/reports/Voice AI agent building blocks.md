# Cascades still carry production voice agents

As of October 2026, most production voice agents are still **streaming cascades** (speech-to-text → text LLM → text-to-speech) running inside an orchestration framework or a managed platform. The text layer is where tool calling, model choice, voice choice, auditable transcripts and per-language quality all live. Native speech-to-speech (STS) models are better on latency and prosody, and the frontier is moving toward a **hybrid**: a full-duplex voice front end that hands reasoning and tools to a text model. OpenAI's GPT-Live-1 (API launch 2026-09-10) is the clearest example. By OpenAI's own figures it nearly doubles task completion over its single-model realtime predecessor (83.6% vs 45.7% on Tau3). The harder engineering problem is turn-taking, not model choice. Endpointing silence and LLM first-token time take most of a 0.8–1.5 s budget, and the largest vendor production dataset puts real median voice-to-voice latency at **1.4–1.7 s**, about twice the "800 ms" target everyone quotes. Measured voice agents keep only **30–45% of the task capability** the same models show in text (τ-Voice, ICML 2026), so evaluation has to run on audio, include noise, and score tasks. On cost, STT plus TTS comes to about **$0.02 per conversation-minute**, and telephony adds $0.01–0.02. The managed platform's orchestration fee ($0.05–0.08/min) is the main thing a build saves, which puts the cost break-even in the tens of thousands of minutes a month. It comes sooner when compliance features such as HIPAA or EU residency are sold only through sales contracts. Telephony is cheap but legally heavy. In the US, an AI voice on an outbound call counts as an "artificial voice" under the TCPA. Since 2 August 2026, the EU AI Act has required AI disclosure at the first interaction. Hungarian customer-service lines in scope must record every call and offer a live human as the first menu option. Hungarian narrows every layer. Only a few STT and TTS options document `hu`, Gemini Live is the only native STS that does, and none of the leading turn detectors or open duplex models confirm it. A Hungarian agent therefore starts as a cascade with its own Hungarian test set.

*How to read this report.* It feeds a skill pack that teaches patterns, so each section leads with **durable method**: decision rules, failure modes, metrics and cost arithmetic that should outlast the current vendor lineup. Model names, prices and statuses are **dated vendor facts**. Unless a different date is given, each was checked on the vendor's own page on 2026-10-17, and they are collected in the ledger near the end for refreshing. "Vendor-reported" means no independent measurement was found. Arithmetic marked as derived is this report's own calculation from cited prices, not a vendor figure.

## Choose a cascade by default and native speech-to-speech only where feel outranks control

The architecture decision comes down to a few rules that the 2026 evidence supports from several directions. **Choose a cascade** when the product needs any of these: a specific or brand-cloned voice, strict tool and argument accuracy, text transcripts as the system of record, best-in-class STT/TTS for a lower-resource language, or the freedom to swap the LLM. **Choose native STS** when sub-second turn-taking and responsiveness to emotion and prosody drive the product (companions, coaching, consumer assistants), tools are few and simple, the language is well covered, and the vendor's fixed voice catalog is acceptable. Practitioner and vendor sources agree that cascades still dominate production. Typical first-audio time is quoted at **600–1,200 ms for cascades and 300–500 ms for STS**. A well-optimized streaming cascade narrows the gap without closing it, and the cascade throws away prosody at the STT step ([FutureAGI](https://futureagi.com/blog/cascaded-voice-ai-vs-speech-to-speech-2026/); [Soniox wiki](https://soniox.com/wiki/speech-to-speech-models-vs-pipelines)). Nearly all of these comparisons come from parties selling one side. The sharpest argument comes from Gradium, a cascade STT/TTS seller whose co-founders run Kyutai, the lab behind the main open duplex model. Gradium says "tool use, function calling and structured output are solved problems in the text layer," that prompt techniques "don't transfer directly" to STS, and that in STS "the cost to switch the underlying text model is extremely high" ([Gradium](https://gradium.ai/content/cascaded-voice-agent-vs-speech-to-speech-2026)).

The strongest evidence for the cascade comes from an STS vendor. OpenAI's own benchmark puts its single-model **GPT-Realtime-2.1 at 45.7% first-attempt completion on Tau3**, against **83.6%** when the duplex GPT-Live-1 front end delegates to a GPT-6 Astra text backend. Turn-taking latency also falls, from 1.41 s to 0.798 s (vendor-reported, [OpenAI Developer Community](https://community.openai.com/t/introducing-gpt-live-1-in-the-api/1396471)). The open-model frontier shows the same weakness. NVIDIA's NemotronLabs VoiceChat, the first open full-duplex model with native tool calling, reports 82.5% tool-selection F1 and names "argument accuracy and end-to-end tool execution" as unsolved ([arXiv 2609.21967](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.21967)). The vendor direction across the field is the hybrid. GPT-Live offers "Responses delegation" (OpenAI runs the backend) or "client delegation" (you bring your own model or agent), and "backend work can continue when the caller interrupts" ([OpenAI GPT-Live guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/live)). Gemini 3.8 Live makes NON_BLOCKING asynchronous function calls the default ([Gemini Live guide](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/live-guide)). Amazon's Nova 2 Sonic lets "the assistant continue speaking while tools process" ([AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/nova/latest/nova2-userguide/using-conversational-speech.html)). Azure Voice Live serves native realtime models and cascaded STT→LLM→TTS behind one API ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-services/speech-service/voice-live)). Both major frameworks make the hybrid practical at the node level. LiveKit lets a handoff switch an agent "to Realtime API from standard LLMs" ([livekit/agents](https://github.com/livekit/agents)), and Pipecat ships OpenAI realtime delegation examples ([Pipecat CHANGELOG](https://github.com/pipecat-ai/pipecat/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md)). The durable rule is to put STS on open conversational nodes and keep tool-heavy or compliance-sensitive nodes on a cascade or a delegated text model.

STS brings its own failure modes, and an agent design has to budget for each one. **Session and connection caps** force reconnect logic from day one. OpenAI Realtime sessions end at 60 minutes ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/realtime-conversations)). Gemini Live audio-only sessions stop at 15 minutes without context compression, connections last "around 10 minutes," and resumption tokens stay valid for 2 hours ([Google](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/live-session)). Nova 2 Sonic caps connections at 8 minutes ([AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/nova/latest/nova2-userguide/using-conversational-speech.html)). **Voice is locked** once the session emits audio: "the voice cannot be modified for that session" ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/realtime-conversations)). **Audio and transcript can diverge.** Forum reports describe the model speaking sentences that never appear in logged transcripts ([OpenAI forum](https://community.openai.com/t/hallucination-from-realtime-audio-api/1113538)). A user also disputes OpenAI's claim that GPT-Live provides user transcripts, and that dispute is unresolved ([OpenAI forum](https://community.openai.com/t/introducing-gpt-live-1-in-the-api/1396471)). An STS agent whose transcript is its audit record therefore needs an independent STT tap. **Interruption semantics differ by vendor.** Gemini cancels and discards the generation ([Google](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/live-guide)), GPT-Live keeps backend work running unless the app cancels it ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/live)), and OpenAI's WebSocket path needs the client to send `conversation.item.truncate`. Without it the model's context holds audio the user never heard ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/realtime-conversations)). **Reasoning costs latency and money**: OpenAI warns that higher effort "can raise latency and output token usage," and Gemini bills thinking at output rates ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/gpt-realtime-2.1); [Google pricing](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing)). Inside frameworks the STS path is less battle-tested. Pipecat's 2026 changelog keeps fixing STS-specific tool-call, transcript and sample-rate bugs, and text-domain features such as LiveKit's `min_words` interruption filter need a parallel STT ([Pipecat CHANGELOG](https://github.com/pipecat-ai/pipecat/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md); [LiveKit Turns](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/logic/turns/)).

The billing unit sets the shape of STS cost. **Token-billed STS re-reads the accumulated conversation on every response**, so if the history is not truncated, total input grows roughly with the square of call length (derived). That is why cache pricing dominates. For gpt-realtime-2.1, cached audio input costs $0.40/1M against $32/1M uncached, an 80× spread ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/gpt-realtime-2.1)). Third-party estimates put the flagship at $0.06–0.11/min with working caching and $0.15–0.24/min without ([Fora Soft](https://www.forasoft.com/blog/article/openai-realtime-api-pricing)). The base conversion uses Azure's documented rate of about 10 audio tokens/s in and 20/s out ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-services/speech-service/voice-live)). That gives **≈$0.019 per minute of user audio and ≈$0.077 per minute of model audio** on gpt-realtime-2.1 before any re-reads (derived). **Per-minute billing caps the growth**: GPT-Live-1 at $0.05/min for the voice layer plus backend tokens, Grok Voice at a flat $0.08/min, and Gemini's per-minute audio rates. This report found no vendor post-mortem documenting a context-growth cost blowup, so the quadratic growth is inferred from the billing model.

Open, self-hostable duplex models are not yet a production option for tool-using agents. The lineage is still **Kyutai Moshi** (2024; models CC-BY-4.0, about 24 GB VRAM in PyTorch, "as low as 200 ms on an L4" vendor-reported) ([kyutai-labs/moshi](https://github.com/kyutai-labs/moshi)). Derivatives include NVIDIA's PersonaPlex-7B (English only, A100/H100, NVIDIA Open Model License, no tool calling) ([HF nvidia/personaplex-7b-v1](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/personaplex-7b-v1)) and Kyutai's GRPO-tuned "seamless" variants, which target pause handling and backchanneling ([arXiv 2606.11167](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.11167v1)). All of them are effectively English-only and need datacenter GPUs, and per-GPU concurrency is undocumented. Self-hosting STS makes sense only for English, or with your own fine-tuning.

## Turn-taking spends most of the latency budget

Define latency the way the caller experiences it: **from the end of the user's speech to the first agent audio byte heard** (`firstAudioByte − userSpeechEnd`), reported at p50, p95 and p99 ([Hamming](https://hamming.ai/resources/voice-ai-latency-whats-fast-whats-slow-how-to-fix-it)). Never compute it by adding up stage medians. Stages overlap in a streaming pipeline, and LiveKit's own dashboards warn that stage segments "don't sum to the p50 or p95 values" ([LiveKit observability](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/observability/)). Adding up vendor-reported component figures does give a useful budget (derived): endpointing (a 200 ms VAD stop plus about 50 ms of semantic turn model, or a 500 ms minimum delay) + STT finalization (about 150 ms) + LLM time-to-first-token (300–600 ms) + TTS first byte (75–150 ms) + network (50–100 ms) comes to **about 0.8–1.5 s**. Endpointing and the LLM dominate. Practitioners aim for **sub-800 ms p95 and sub-500 ms median** ([Hamming](https://hamming.ai/blog/voice-ai-latency-whats-fast-whats-slow-how-to-fix-it); [twig.so](https://www.twig.so/blog/voice-ai-agents-latency-budget-800ms)). Reality looks different. Hamming's aggregate across "10M+ mins … 10K+ voice agents" reports **P50 1.4–1.7 s, P95 4.3–5.4 s and P99 8.4–15.3 s** (vendor, sampling method unstated) ([Hamming](https://hamming.ai/resources/voice-ai-latency-whats-fast-whats-slow-how-to-fix-it)). Coval proposes a more realistic p95 target under 1.5 s ([Coval](https://www.coval.ai/blog/how-to-measure-voice-ai-latency-the-complete-guide/)). Two things follow for a skill pack. Published latency numbers are best-case lab figures, so teams should report their own percentiles measured at the ear. And the tails, driven by cold starts (500–2,000 ms on serverless), mobile networks and provider queueing, are where the product is actually experienced ([Hamming](https://hamming.ai/resources/voice-ai-latency-whats-fast-whats-slow-how-to-fix-it)). One team's open benchmark found that **TTS has the most variance of any stage, and that it changes with time of day** ([vox-bench](https://dev.to/autor_tech/were-open-sourcing-our-voice-ai-latency-benchmarking-tool-3oa8)).

The standard 2026 endpointing stack is **Silero VAD plus a semantic end-of-turn model**. The model comes in one of three architectures, and the choice decides language coverage and portability. **Audio-native classifiers** read prosody directly. Pipecat's Smart Turn v3.x is BSD-2, about 8M parameters, and covers 23 languages, with weights, data and training code all open. It runs "in as little as 10ms on some CPUs" and only during silence, and v3.2 cut misclassifications of short replies like "yes" by about 40% on the project's own benchmark ([smart-turn](https://github.com/pipecat-ai/smart-turn); [Daily](https://www.daily.co/blog/smart-turn-v3-2-handling-noisy-environments-and-short-responses/)). LiveKit's current `TurnDetector` (v1 on LiveKit Inference, v1-mini local) also "encodes user audio directly" and covers 14 languages ([LiveKit turn detector](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/logic/turns/turn-detector/)). **Text-based detectors**, such as LiveKit's previous Qwen2.5-0.5B `MultilingualModel`, cannot use pauses or intonation and depend on the STT's language ID. LiveKit has deprecated that model, and it is "slated for removal in version 2.0" ([LiveKit](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/logic/turns/turn-detector/)). **STT-native turn detection** builds the decision into the recognizer, as in Deepgram Flux and Speechmatics Linden 1 ([Deepgram](https://developers.deepgram.com/docs/models-languages-overview); [Speechmatics](https://www.speechmatics.com/pricing)). Every family exposes the same trade-off as thresholds. Flux's `eot_threshold` defaults to 0.7, and raising it means fewer false turn-ends but more latency ([Deepgram Flux configuration](https://developers.deepgram.com/docs/flux/configuration)). LiveKit's endpointing delays default to 0.5 s minimum and 6.0 s maximum ([LiveKit](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/logic/turns/turn-detector)). Gemini recommends a silence duration of 500–800 ms ([Google](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/live-guide)). In every case "every millisecond of that silence timer adds straight to response time" ([Soniox wiki](https://soniox.com/wiki/voice-agent-latency-budget)).

Speculative ("eager") end-of-turn is the main remaining latency lever, and it **turns latency into LLM spend**. Deepgram says eager EOT saves "hundreds of milliseconds" but increases LLM calls by **50–70%**, so a cascade cost model should multiply the LLM line by about 1.5–1.7 when it is on ([Deepgram](https://developers.deepgram.com/docs/flux/configuration)). Telnyx turns it on by default for Flux and reports about 150 ms lower median latency (vendor) ([Telnyx](https://telnyx.com/release-notes/automatic-eager-end-of-turn-deepgram-flux)). A second lever gets less attention: **whether the STT supports explicit finalization**. Daily's open benchmark of 10 STT services found that services with finalization respond at about median latency, while services without it force the pipeline to wait about p95 on **every** turn ([Daily](https://www.daily.co/blog/benchmarking-stt-for-voice-agents/)). That makes finalization support a top selection criterion. A practitioner rule of thumb follows from the same observation: before tuning VAD, check STT partial latency, because "if Deepgram is taking >400ms for partials, the problem isn't VAD" (anecdotal) ([DEV](https://dev.to/callstacktech/implementing-vad-and-turn-taking-for-natural-voice-ai-flow-my-experience-1bdf)).

Interruption handling rests on one invariant: **the conversation history must hold only what the caller actually heard**. Each stack exposes the primitive differently. OpenAI WebSocket clients stop playback and send `conversation.item.truncate`, while WebRTC truncates on the server ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/realtime-conversations)). Twilio Media Streams pairs `clear` (flush buffered audio) with `mark` (which reports how much audio played) ([Twilio](https://www.twilio.com/docs/voice/media-streams/websocket-messages)). LiveKit "truncates history to what the user actually heard" by default ([LiveKit Turns](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/logic/turns/)). Measure barge-in quality as **two opposing error rates**. A **false yield** is the agent stopping for a backchannel or noise. A **swallowed interruption** is the agent talking over a real barge-in. Raising sensitivity trades one for the other ([Deepgram](https://deepgram.com/learn/backchannels-vs-interruptions-voice-agents)). The baseline shows why a single "interruption rate" means nothing. In Alibaba's production system, a rule-based policy that treated confident ASR partials as interruptions was **89% false barge-ins and 11% real**, and the authors traced the false ones to four causes: backchannels, noise, the agent hearing its own echo, and misplaced turns ([arXiv 2205.15060](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.15060)). All three acoustic causes look identical in logs, so the order of work is: get echo cancellation right, label a sample of interruptions by cause, and only then tune thresholds. Current mitigations include LiveKit's adaptive interruption mode, which "can distinguish true interruptions from conversational backchanneling" and resumes after false interruptions ([LiveKit Turns](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/logic/turns/)), and Pipecat's mute strategies and interruption defaults ([Pipecat](https://docs.pipecat.ai/pipecat/fundamentals/interruptions)). The recurring bugs are mechanical. Configuring both native and manual interruption handlers produces double audio. An unflushed TTS buffer keeps the agent talking for 2–3 s after a barge-in. A VAD threshold of 0.3 triggers on breathing ([DEV](https://dev.to/callstacktech/implementing-real-time-streaming-with-vapi-build-voice-apps-3ehg); [DEV](https://dev.to/callstacktech/implementing-vad-and-turn-taking-for-natural-voice-ai-flow-my-experience-1bdf), both anecdotal). In loud environments there is no real silence, so silence-based endpointing falls back to timeouts and latency goes up ([Voiceflow docs mirror](https://glama.ai/mcp/servers/@florinel-chis/voiceflow-doc-mcp/blob/dd1eccac05536155da291f9fb61c82caa8b32f16/data/voiceflow_docs/voice-input.md)).

## Pick speech components on language, finalization and billing unit, not leaderboard rank

For a cascade, the selection criteria that change outcomes are mostly not the headline accuracy number. For **STT**, check six things. Does it truly stream partials over a socket? Does it finalize explicitly? Does it have built-in turn detection? Which languages does each *mode* cover? What is the billing unit? What is the concurrency ceiling? Language coverage differs inside a single vendor. Deepgram Nova-3 lists Hungarian as a monolingual code, but its code-switching `multi` mode and both Flux models cover only ten languages, without `hu` ([Deepgram](https://developers.deepgram.com/docs/models-languages-overview)). Billing units hide surprises. AssemblyAI bills streaming on **session duration, including idle socket time** ([AssemblyAI](https://www.assemblyai.com/pricing)). Deepgram bills "true per-second" ([Deepgram](https://deepgram.com/pricing)). Groq Whisper is file-based with a 10-second minimum per request, so it does not stream at all ([Groq](https://groq.com/pricing)). Independent benchmarks now exist but carry caveats. Artificial Analysis runs a separate *streaming* STT leaderboard whose latency clock starts at Silero-detected end of speech, with WER weighted over agent-talk, VoxPopuli and earnings-call audio ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/speech-to-text/streaming)). Daily's open benchmark, written by a framework vendor, ran 1,000 English samples through a real Pipecat pipeline at real-time pace and found **Deepgram Nova-3 (247 ms / 1.62% WER), Soniox (249 ms / 1.29%) and Speechmatics (495 ms / 1.07%)** on the latency–accuracy Pareto frontier. Its LLM-judged "Semantic WER", which counts an error only if the agent would respond differently, stayed under 4.4% for every service ([Daily](https://www.daily.co/blog/benchmarking-stt-for-voice-agents/)). The durable lesson in Semantic WER is that once most services clear a threshold, raw WER differences matter less than latency, finalization and language. Both benchmarks are English-centric, which matters for Hungarian.

For **TTS**, select on first-byte latency, language, voice ownership and consent, text normalization, and vendor survival. The last two are where production failures come from. ElevenLabs warns that phone numbers, zip codes and emails "might be mispronounced" and recommends preprocessing ([ElevenLabs](https://docs.cdntest.elevenlabs.io/best-practices/prompting/normalization)). One case study describes an agent misreading tracking numbers after the TTS provider silently changed its default normalization ([Dograh](https://www.dograh.com/feeds/blog/tts-number-formatting)). The defence is **deterministic normalization in your own code before TTS** plus audio-level regression tests that run even when your code has not changed. Vendor survival is a real risk. PlayHT's API went offline in July 2025 and the service closed on 31 December 2025 with clones deleted ([anyspeech](https://anyspeech.io/playht-alternatives)). Pipecat 1.10.0 removed its LMNT TTS service because "LMNT has shut down" ([Pipecat CHANGELOG](https://github.com/pipecat-ai/pipecat/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md)). The pattern is to keep the original consented voice recordings on your side and hide the TTS vendor behind an abstraction. Cloning carries policy constraints. ElevenLabs admits only human-verified professional clones to its shared library and treats evasion of its verification safeguards as grounds for a ban ([ElevenLabs help](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/help-center/product/voices/voice-library/can-i-share-an-ai-generated-voice-in-the-voice-library)). The FCC treats voice clones as "artificial" voices ([FCC 24-17](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-24-17A1.pdf)).

Speech-layer cost reduces to one formula (derived): **STT rate × full call minutes + (agent talk share × ~800 characters per minute × TTS rate per character)**. STT bills for the whole call because agents stream continuously. Cartesia states that a minute of generated audio takes 750–800 characters ([Cartesia](https://cartesia.ai/pricing)). If the agent speaks half the time, TTS covers about 400 characters per conversation-minute. At list prices on 2026-10-17, the budget English stack of Deepgram Nova-3 ($0.0048/min, a promotional rate against a $0.0077 list) plus Aura-2 ($0.030/1K characters) comes to **about $0.017/min**. Flux English plus ElevenLabs Flash ($0.04/1K) comes to about $0.0225/min. A Hungarian-capable pair of Nova-3 `hu` or Scribe v2 Realtime with ElevenLabs Flash v2.5 comes to about **$0.021–0.0225/min** ([Deepgram](https://deepgram.com/pricing); [ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/pricing/api)). On top of the speech layer come the LLM (enable eager EOT and its line grows by 1.5–1.7×), telephony ($0.01–0.02/min, below), hosting ($0.01/min on Pipecat Cloud agent-1x or LiveKit Cloud overage) ([Daily](https://www.daily.co/pricing/pipecat-cloud/); [LiveKit](https://livekit.com/pricing)), and any platform fee. A self-built cascade on US telephony with a small LLM therefore lands at **roughly $0.05–0.07 per minute before engineering time** (derived; Retell's per-minute LLM list prices of $0.003–0.016 for small models are used as the LLM proxy, [Retell](https://www.retellai.com/pricing)). Cerebrium's vendor measurement of a self-hosted serverless-GPU stack, at $0.029/min for STT+LLM+TTS, fits that range ([Cerebrium](https://cerebrium.ai/resources/voice-ai-agent-cost-per-minute)).

Open-weight speech models are licence-sensitive and language-thin, so check the licence file, not the badge. NVIDIA's Parakeet-TDT-0.6B-v3 and Canary-1B-v2 are CC-BY-4.0 and list Hungarian among 25 European languages, but as packaged they are offline models that need a chunked or streaming runner ([HF Parakeet](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v3); [HF Canary](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/canary-1b-v2)). NVIDIA's own English streaming model ships under an "other" licence ([HF](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/nemotron-speech-streaming-en-0.6b)). None of the open TTS models checked list Hungarian: Kokoro (Apache-2.0, `en`), Orpheus (Apache-2.0), Sesame CSM-1B (Apache-2.0, gated), Chatterbox (MIT, 23 languages without `hu`) and Kyutai TTS (CC-BY-4.0, en/fr) ([HF Chatterbox](https://huggingface.co/ResembleAI/chatterbox); [HF Kokoro](https://huggingface.co/hexgrad/Kokoro-82M)).

## The orchestration fee is the whole build-versus-buy margin

The two maintained open frameworks differ in their center of gravity more than in what they can do. **Pipecat** (BSD-2, copyright Daily) is pipeline-first, built from composable frame processors. It ships WebRTC transports (Daily, LiveKit, SmallWebRTC, Vonage), raw WebSocket serializers for Twilio, Telnyx, Plivo, Vonage, Exotel and Genesys, about 25 LLM providers and six STS services ([Pipecat README](https://github.com/pipecat-ai/pipecat)). **LiveKit Agents** (Apache-2.0) runs agents as programmable participants in WebRTC rooms. It integrates SIP, a default turn detector, adaptive interruption, MCP, handoffs and a pytest judge framework ([livekit/agents](https://github.com/livekit/agents)). The independent verdicts agree. Thoughtworks put Pipecat in **Assess** in April 2026, citing "a less integrated production path" and the need for "significant platform engineering work" before business-critical use ([Thoughtworks Radar](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/languages-and-frameworks/pipecat)). WebRTC.ventures says LiveKit suits WebRTC-first and SIP-heavy deployments "without building a separate media stack," while Pipecat suits custom, self-hosted, telephony-WebSocket and vendor-neutral builds ([WebRTC.ventures](https://webrtc.ventures/2026/03/choosing-a-voice-ai-agent-production-framework/)). The durable rule is to **let the audio transport drive the choice**, and to keep the "intelligence layer" (prompts, tools, memory, knowledge bases, monitoring) outside the framework, so that a later switch rewrites only the pipeline and transport wrapper ([Chanl](https://www.channel.tel/blog/pipecat-vs-livekit-voice-framework-decision)). The two are not mutually exclusive, since Pipecat ships a LiveKit transport ([Pipecat README](https://github.com/pipecat-ai/pipecat)). Comparison content needs scepticism. A widely circulated vendor blog describes Smart Turn as transcript-based and LiveKit as VAD-silence-based, and the primary docs contradict both claims ([smart-turn](https://github.com/pipecat-ai/smart-turn); [LiveKit Turns](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/logic/turns/)).

Lock-in sits in licences and hosted features more than in framework code, and a decision record should name each point. LiveKit's turn-detector weights carry a **MODEL_LICENSE that allows use "only together with the LiveKit Agents framework"** and claims derivatives as LiveKit Models ([MODEL_LICENSE](https://github.com/livekit/agents/blob/main/MODEL_LICENSE)). Adaptive interruption is the default only on LiveKit Cloud, and evals and observability are Cloud products metered per turn and per event ([LiveKit Turns](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/logic/turns/); [LiveKit pricing](https://livekit.com/pricing)). On Pipecat Cloud with Daily telephony, a bridged transfer keeps billing because "the original call stays anchored to Daily", while a SIP REFER removes Daily from the call once the handoff succeeds ([Daily](https://www.daily.co/pricing/pipecat-cloud/); corrected 2026-10-17, see Errata). Flow definitions are framework DSLs, although Pipecat 1.9.0's `FlowConfig` JSON/YAML schema makes flows portable as data ([Pipecat CHANGELOG](https://github.com/pipecat-ai/pipecat/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md)). Agora's TEN Framework is "Apache-2.0 plus additional conditions" that forbid hosting it on end-user devices and any deployment "that competes with Agora's offerings." That rules it out for on-device agents and for agencies or platforms that let others deploy ([TEN LICENSE](https://github.com/TEN-framework/ten-framework/blob/main/LICENSE)). Fast release cadence is its own risk. Pipecat shipped 12 minor versions between 1.0 (2026-04-14) and 1.12.0 (2026-09-25), including breaking changes inside minor releases, so **pin framework versions in production** ([Pipecat CHANGELOG](https://github.com/pipecat-ai/pipecat/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md)). For the long tail of alternatives: Vocode is unmaintained (last push 2024-11-15; its README asks for community maintainers). Ultravox's repo is a speech-LLM model, not a framework. Bolna and Jambonz are active telephony-centric stacks. OpenAI's Agents SDK `VoicePipeline` is a thin STT→workflow→TTS wrapper with "no built-in interruption handling" for streamed input and no transport layer ([vocode-core](https://github.com/vocodedev/vocode-core); [ultravox](https://github.com/fixie-ai/ultravox); [OpenAI Agents SDK](https://openai.github.io/openai-agents-python/voice/pipeline/)).

Managed platforms fall into three pricing shapes, and the shape predicts where costs hide. **Unbundled orchestrators** charge a platform fee and pass through components: Vapi at "$0.05/min Vapi hosting" with models "pass-through at cost," Retell at a $0.055/min infrastructure fee ([Vapi](https://vapi.ai/pricing); [Retell](https://www.retellai.com/pricing)). **Bundled per-minute vendors** fold in components: ElevenLabs Agents at $0.08/min with LLM and telephony on top, Bland at $0.12–0.14/min including LLM, STT and TTS ([ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/pricing/api); [Bland](https://www.bland.ai/pricing)). **Sales-led enterprise vendors** publish no per-minute rate: Synthflow contracts start at $30,000/year, and PolyAI and Sierra list none ([Synthflow](https://synthflow.ai/pricing); [PolyAI](https://poly.ai/pricing)). At list prices a realistic US PSTN minute with a mid-tier LLM costs **about $0.09–0.15 all-in**, and vendors' own headline examples assume zero telephony ([Vapi](https://vapi.ai/pricing); [Retell](https://www.retellai.com/pricing)). The surprises are structural. Concurrency costs extra above the tier: ElevenLabs bills **burst minutes at 2× ($0.16)**, Vapi charges $10 per extra line per month, Retell $8 per concurrency above 20. Retell bills silence and hold, and keeps telephony running after a transfer. Feature metering adds up: Retell's QA costs $0.10/min, PII removal $0.01/min, and knowledge base use $0.005/min. Premium voices cost up to 6.7× platform voices, and Retell's "Fast tier" doubles the LLM price ([ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/pricing/api); [Vapi](https://vapi.ai/pricing); [Retell](https://www.retellai.com/pricing)). The **LLM and TTS choice moves the per-minute price more than the platform choice does**. On Retell, moving from GPT 5 mini to Claude 5.5 Sonnet adds $0.056/min, which is more than the entire infrastructure fee ([Retell](https://www.retellai.com/pricing)).

Compliance is sold, not configured, and for many buyers that is the real build trigger. Vapi's HIPAA add-on costs **$2,000/month**, which adds about $0.10/min at 20,000 minutes (derived). Its **EU region is available only on the custom-priced Premier tier**, as an isolated environment whose resources cannot move between regions. Raw-data retention rises with tier: 14, then 30, then 180 days ([Vapi](https://vapi.ai/pricing); [Vapi EU region](https://docs.vapi.ai/security-and-privacy/eu-region.md)). Vapi's Zero Data Retention and HIPAA modes are mutually exclusive. Once ZDR is on, "any call content you do not send to your own systems cannot be recovered," so the export path is an end-of-call webhook into your own bucket, set up *before* ZDR is enabled ([Vapi ZDR](https://docs.vapi.ai/security-and-privacy/zero-data-retention.md)). ElevenLabs offers Zero Retention Mode and US/EU/India residency only under enterprise controls ([ElevenLabs Agents](https://elevenlabs.io/agents)). The build-versus-buy arithmetic follows (derived). A build saves at most the platform fee minus self-hosting, about **$0.04–0.07/min**: roughly $400–700 a month at 10,000 minutes, which is less than a fraction of an engineer, and $4,000–7,000 at 100,000 minutes, which can fund one. Vendor-adjacent comparison blogs put the break-even anywhere from about 10,000 to 50,000+ minutes a month, with claimed savings of 60–80% that only hold if the build also changes STT/TTS/LLM providers ([particula](https://particula.tech/blog/vapi-vs-retell-vs-livekit-vs-pipecat-voice-agent-platform); [samcom](https://www.samcomtechnologies.com/blog/vapi-vs-livekit-for-ai-voice-agents-in-2026-a-developer-head-to-head)). No first-hand migration post-mortems were found. The decision rule is: **buy to learn, and build when volume passes the tens of thousands of minutes, when EU residency or HIPAA would be sales-gated, when turn-taking has to beat the platform's floor, or when the language forces component choices the platform does not offer**. Middle paths make the exit gradual. Twilio ConversationRelay ($0.07/min) and Telnyx Conversation Relay ($0.05/min) run telephony and speech while you own the LLM loop. Twilio Media Streams ($0.0044/min) hands raw audio to your own Pipecat or LiveKit pipeline ([Twilio](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/voice/pricing/us); [Telnyx](https://telnyx.com/pricing/call-control)). Lock-in is lowest when telephony is BYO, because Vapi, Retell and Bland all accept your own SIP trunk or numbers, and a migration then only re-points the trunk.

## Telephony is cheap, but the law treats outbound AI calls as robocalls

There are two engineering routes onto the phone network. The first is a **programmable-voice media stream** from a CPaaS. Twilio's is base64 μ-law at 8 kHz mono, bidirectional only under `<Connect><Stream>`, with `clear` and `mark` for barge-in and DTMF on the inbound track ([Twilio Media Streams](https://www.twilio.com/docs/voice/media-streams/websocket-messages)). The second is **raw SIP** into your own media server or directly into a model endpoint, for example OpenAI's `sip.api.openai.com` (EU: `sip-eu.api.openai.com`), with webhook accept/refer/hangup ([OpenAI SIP](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/realtime-sip)). Telephony audio is band-limited 8 kHz G.711. Models that expect 16/24 kHz PCM need transcoding and resampling at the edge, and 8 kHz input lowers STT accuracy ([Smallest.ai](https://smallest.ai/blog/text-to-speech-for-phone-calls-latency-telephony-audio-and-turn-taking)). CPaaS products that emit 16 kHz linear PCM (Plivo, Vonage) remove the decode step but not the band limit ([Plivo](https://plivo.com/docs/voice-agents/audio-streaming/concepts/audio-streaming-guide)). Vendor estimates of PSTN/SIP latency, all self-interested, are 30–120 ms one-way plus 40–100 ms of WebRTC jitter buffering ([ConnexCS](https://connexcs.com/blog/webrtc-vs-sip-for-ai-voice-agents-which-architecture-scales)). At list price the telephony leg is small: outbound plus media stream plus recording costs **about $0.021/min on Twilio and $0.0125/min on Telnyx** (derived from [Twilio](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/voice/pricing/us) and [Telnyx](https://telnyx.com/pricing/call-control)). Non-US and Hungarian carrier rates were not collected. The operational traps are elsewhere. Answering-machine detection makes no accuracy promise ("will not always return the right answer"). Its blocking mode's 2,400 ms default speech threshold runs into the US 2-second abandonment rule, so outbound flows should use **async AMD plus an immediate compliant greeting**, and AMD shares Twilio's budget of four forked audio streams per call ([Twilio AMD](https://www.twilio.com/docs/voice/answering-machine-detection)). Caller-ID reputation depends on STIR/SHAKEN attestation and on spam labels from three analytics vendors (Hiya, TNS, First Orion), and registration through Free Caller Registry has to be renewed every 180 days ([SignalWire](https://signalwire.com/docs/platform/voice/resolving-spam-labels.md)). Transfers come in two forms. **Cold** transfer is a single REFER or `<Dial>`. **Warm** transfer has to be orchestrated: conference the human in, brief them, drop the AI leg. It adds 15–30 s, needs hold audio, and needs a spoken recovery message if the SIP transfer fails ([ConnexCS handoff guide](https://connexcs.com/blog/the-ai-voice-agent-handoff-guide-triggers-context-and-sip-infrastructure)). The handoff payload should carry a summary, the caller's intent, the data already collected and the escalation reason ([Retell](https://www.retellai.com/nl/blog/can-voicebots-escalate-calls-to-human-agents)).

**United States.** FCC Declaratory Ruling 24-17 (effective 8 February 2024) holds that the TCPA's "artificial or prerecorded voice" covers AI-generated and cloned voices. Outbound AI calls therefore need prior express consent, and prior express *written* consent for telemarketing ([FCC 24-17](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-24-17A1.pdf)). The current 47 CFR 64.1200 sets the engineering defaults ([eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-64/subpart-L/section-64.1200)). The caller must state the registered business identity at the beginning and give a callback number. Telemarketing calls must offer an automated voice or key-press opt-out **within 2 seconds** of the identification; the opt-out adds the number to the DNC list and ends the call. Consent can be revoked "by using any reasonable method" and must be honored within 10 business days. Calls are allowed only between 8 a.m. and 9 p.m. at the callee's location, with National DNC scrubbing. Abandoned calls are capped at 3% per 30-day campaign, where a call counts as abandoned if no "live sales representative" connects within 2 s of the greeting. The 2023 one-to-one consent rule was vacated on 24 January 2025 and is absent from the current text ([Morrison Foerster](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/250130-eleventh-circuit-vacates-fcc-s-tcpa-one-to-one-consent-rule)). The FCC's AI-specific disclosure proposal (FCC 24-84, August 2024) remained **proposed, not final** as of 2026-10-17. That proposal itself notes that the TCPA "do[es] not extend to technologies used to answer inbound calls" ([Federal Register](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-09-10/html/2024-19028.htm)). The FTC's Telemarketing Sales Rule adds an express written agreement, a 15-second / 4-ring minimum and a 2-second disclosure for prerecorded sales calls ([eCFR 16 CFR 310.4](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-310/section-310.4)). State law layers on more. California AB 2905 (effective 1 January 2025) requires disclosure of artificial voices in autodialed messages ([Loeb & Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/passle/2024/12/california-enacts-18-new-ai-laws-what-advertisers-need-to-know)). A 2026–2027 wave of chatbot laws includes Oregon and Washington statutes effective 1 January 2027 with private rights of action ([Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/04/2026-State-Chatbot-Laws-Key-Provisions-and-Regulatory-Trends)). About 11–13 states require all-party consent for recording ([KTS Law](https://ktslaw.com/en/Blog/GlobalPrivacy-and-CybersecurityLaw/2024/7/Wiretap-Laws-in-the-United-States)). One question is unresolved and should go to counsel: whether an AI agent can ever count as the "live sales representative" that satisfies the abandonment rule. The conservative reading of FCC 24-17 is that it cannot.

**EU, UK and Hungary.** EU AI Act **Art. 50(1) has applied since 2 August 2026**. Providers of systems "intended to interact directly with natural persons" must make sure people are told they are dealing with an AI "at the latest at the time of the first interaction," unless that is obvious. Breaches fall in the tier of up to **€15M or 3%** of worldwide turnover ([AI Act Art. 50](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/); [Art. 99](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/99/)). The Digital Omnibus (Reg. (EU) 2026/1744, in force 27 July 2026) did not postpone Art. 50(1). It deferred only machine-readable marking for systems already on the market (to 2 December 2026) and the high-risk obligations ([Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/blogs/sourcingatmorganlewis/2026/08/eu-ai-acts-transparency-rules-what-went-into-effect-on-2-august); [Usercentrics](https://usercentrics.com/knowledge-hub/eu-ai-act-high-risk-delay-article-50-transparency-consent/)). If the agent infers emotion from the voice, Art. 50(3) adds a deployer disclosure duty. ePrivacy Art. 13(1) makes automated direct-marketing calls opt-in across the EU ([Directive 2002/58/EC](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eudr/2002/58/article/13)). UK PECR reg. 19 does the same for systems transmitting "sounds which are not live speech," with consent that "must specifically cover automated calls." The ICO says this guidance is under review after the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 ([PECR reg. 19](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2003/2426/regulation/19); [ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guide-to-pecr/electronic-and-telephone-marketing/telephone-marketing/)). The UK's opt-out route for live marketing calls with TPS screening therefore probably does not cover synthetic-voice agents, which needs confirmation. In Hungary, the advertising act (Grt. 6. §) requires "prior, unambiguous and express consent" for direct-contact advertising to natural persons, withdrawable at any time ([njt.hu Grt.](https://njt.hu/jogszabaly/2008-48-00-00)). For in-scope customer-service lines, the consumer protection act (Fgytv. 17/B. §) is unusually operational. The line must answer and start substantive handling **within 5 minutes**. It must put the **live-voice complaint option first in the phone menu**. It must **record all calls**, give each recording a unique ID, keep it **5 years**, and announce this at the start of the call. It must provide a free copy within 30 days on request ([njt.hu Fgytv.](https://njt.hu/jogszabaly/1997-155-00-00)). Sector rules differ. Telecom complaint recordings are kept 2 years under NMHH Decree 2/2015, bank complaint calls 5 years and insurer calls 1 year. NAIH's only recording guidance found predates GDPR (2016) ([NAIH](https://www.naih.hu/files/2016_05_09_tajekoztato_hangfelvetelekrol.pdf)). In practice, an AI agent on such a line sits behind, or alongside, a human-first complaint path.

All of these regimes converge on one call opening and a set of hard invariants, which this report treats as the durable engineering default, subject to legal review in each jurisdiction. The opening states the agent's AI nature, the registered business it calls for, that the call is recorded and why, and how to stop or reach a person. An example: "Hi, this is [name], an AI assistant calling on behalf of [registered business]. This call is recorded for [purpose]. Say 'stop' or press 9 to opt out, or say 'agent' for a person." On outbound telemarketing this has to land inside the 2-second windows, so the **greeting audio is pre-synthesized or cached, never generated after answer**. The invariants are these. Opt-out keywords and DTMF are detected by a **deterministic check outside the LLM**, so the model cannot ignore them. The DNC write completes before hang-up. A consent ledger records who consented, when, the exact disclosure text and the seller. Every utterance and consent event is timestamped. A human-escalation path exists on every call. The agent never claims to be human when asked. Clones of real voices are used only with that person's documented consent. Get legal review before launch for any outbound campaign, any multi-state recording, any EU/UK outbound marketing, regulated sectors, and Hungarian customer-service lines.

## Evaluate at the caller's ear, in noise, against tasks

The metric set that survives scrutiny is short, and each item has a precise definition. **Voice-to-voice latency** is measured at the ear, at p50, p95 and p99, with enough turns that a few outliers do not dominate p95 ([Cekura](https://www.cekura.ai/blogs/voice-ai-latency-guide)). **Turn-taking quality** is the pair of false-yield and swallowed-interruption rates, computed from labeled calls. Pipecat traces carry `turn.was_interrupted` per turn ([Pipecat OpenTelemetry](https://docs.pipecat.ai/server/utilities/opentelemetry)). **Recognition quality** on your own calls is best expressed as task-relevant (semantic) WER ([Daily](https://www.daily.co/blog/benchmarking-stt-for-voice-agents/)). **Task completion** is scored against verifiable state, not judged prose. The reference result is **τ-Voice** (Sierra/Princeton, ICML 2026, 278 tasks). GPT-5 with reasoning reaches 85% pass@1 in text, while voice agents reach **31–51% on clean audio and 26–38% under realistic noise and accents**, keeping 30–45% of text capability, and 79–90% of failures come from agent behavior ([PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v306/ray26a.html)). **Cost per call** can be computed from traces, because Pipecat LLM spans carry token counts and TTS spans carry character counts, and LiveKit emits `lk.agents.usage.tts_characters` ([Pipecat](https://docs.pipecat.ai/server/utilities/opentelemetry); [LiveKit](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/observability/)). Containment, transfer and sentiment rates have no neutral public baselines. Treat them as deployment-specific trends, not as benchmarks. Academic turn-taking benchmarks are maturing. Full-Duplex-Bench v1–v3 covers pause handling through tool use under disfluency ([GitHub](https://github.com/DanielLin94144/Full-Duplex-Bench)), and EchoChain points out that earlier benchmarks score interruption *timing* but not whether the answer after the interruption is correct ([arXiv 2604.16456](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.16456)). A skill should teach both checks.

Testing should be layered by cost and by what each layer can catch, a split LiveKit's own docs make explicit. **Deterministic text-mode unit tests** assert on messages, tool calls and handoffs turn by turn in CI, "a cost-efficient and deterministic way to test agent behavior." **Simulated-caller multi-turn runs** test flows, memory and misuse resistance. **Audio-path runs** with noise, accents and barge-in are kept for turn-taking and ASR/TTS regressions, because they are billed per minute ([LiveKit testing](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/build/testing/)). Clean-audio simulation overstates quality, as τ-Voice's drop from clean to realistic audio shows ([PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v306/ray26a.html)). The hidden variable in every simulation tool is **judge quality**. The only human-validated comparison (21,600 pairwise judgments) found evaluation F1 of 0.92 for one platform against a 0.73 average for two others, but the winning platform's staff co-authored the paper ([arXiv 2511.04133](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.04133); [Speechmatics](https://www.speechmatics.com/company/articles-and-news/de-risk-your-voice-agent-11-best-voice-agent-testing-platforms)). Any adopted judge should therefore be calibrated against a human-labeled sample of your own calls. The commercial category (Hamming, Coval, Cekura, Bluejay, Roark, Evalion, Cyara, plus native Vapi Simulations and LiveKit Agent Simulations at $0.02 per text turn and $0.05 per audio turn) is young, mostly YC-backed and reviewed only by competitors ([Speechmatics](https://www.speechmatics.com/company/articles-and-news/de-risk-your-voice-agent-11-best-voice-agent-testing-platforms); [LiveKit pricing](https://livekit.com/pricing)). Choose on integration and judge calibration, not rankings.

Observability is now OpenTelemetry-native in both frameworks, with two gotchas. Pipecat emits a conversation → turn → `stt`/`llm`/`tts` span tree with `metrics.ttfb`, token and character counts. Its LLM spans carry the serialized prompt **input and output**, which makes the tracing backend a PII store ([Pipecat OpenTelemetry](https://docs.pipecat.ai/server/utilities/opentelemetry)). LiveKit voice spans use `lk.*` attributes, so a filter that keeps only standard `gen_ai.*` spans drops the session and turn spans ([vadim.blog](https://vadim.blog/voice-tracing-compared/)). LiveKit Cloud keeps sessions for 30 days by default, fixes the data region at project creation (EU available), and leaves **PII redaction off by default** ([LiveKit observability](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/observability/)). PII leaks into at least four places: recordings, transcripts, LLM span payloads, and derived artifacts such as summaries. Transcript redaction does not touch the audio. AssemblyAI's audio redaction, for example, works only in English and Spanish and keeps redacted files for only 24 hours ([AssemblyAI PII](https://www.assemblyai.com/docs/pii-redaction)).

Operations converge on a short list of defaults that belong in configuration rather than in backlog tickets. Runaway-cost guards come first: **a hard maximum call duration, an idle timeout when neither side speaks, and voicemail detection**. Without the last one, "every second your agent spends talking to a voicemail inbox is money burned." One vendor reports 80% voicemail detection success ([Zavu](https://docs.zavu.dev/guides/voice-agents/configuration); [ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/blog/voicemail-detection); [Synthflow](https://docs.synthflow.ai/docs/voicemail)). Provider defaults change under deployed agents, so run audio regression suites on a schedule, not only on code changes ([Dograh](https://www.dograh.com/feeds/blog/tts-number-formatting)). Silent degradation is the norm because "nobody listens to the calls," so sample 10–20 failed calls for human listening every week (anecdotal) ([Sessionize](https://sessionize.com/s/abhisheksharma/building-voice-ai-that-doesnt-fall-apart-in-produc/178686)). For accessibility and accuracy, normalize critical data deterministically, read it back in grouped chunks with explicit confirmation, offer DTMF alongside speech for noisy settings and speech impairments, and keep prompts short with a configurable speaking rate. Older IVR studies tie older callers' failures to speech rate, option length and confusing choices ([arXiv 1810.06828](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.06828); [Cognigy](https://www.cognigy.com/glossary/what-is-dtmf); [Vapi](https://docs.vapi.ai/assistants/email-address-reading)). An explicit request for a human should trigger an immediate transfer with no negotiation ([Famulor](https://www.famulor.io/blog/ai-voice-agent-call-transfer-to-a-human-the-2026-guide)).

## Hungarian narrows every layer to two or three options

Hungarian support has to be verified layer by layer, because "90+ languages" claims are often attached to a different model or mode than the one you deploy. In **native STS**, Google is the only major vendor that documents Hungarian: the Gemini Live capability guide lists `hu` among "99 languages," though its overview page says 70 ([Gemini Live guide](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/live-guide); [overview](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/live)). Gemini Live is also the cheapest hosted STS at about $0.005/min audio in and $0.018/min audio out, but it comes with the 10-minute connection and 15-minute session constraints ([Gemini pricing](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing)). None of the OpenAI Realtime or GPT-Live pages fetched lists languages. xAI's realtime docs give no language list. Amazon Nova 2 Sonic supports seven languages without Hungarian. Every open duplex model is English-only or unstated ([AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/nova/latest/nova2-userguide/using-conversational-speech.html); [HF PersonaPlex](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/personaplex-7b-v1)). Azure Voice Live's 140+ STT and 150+ TTS locales very likely include Hungarian, but this report did not check them, and that route is partly cascaded anyway ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-services/speech-service/voice-live)). In **streaming STT**, Hungarian is documented for Deepgram Nova-3 (monolingual `hu` only, not Flux or `multi`), ElevenLabs Scribe v2 Realtime (the 90+ language list includes Hungarian), and open NVIDIA Parakeet/Canary (offline). AssemblyAI's streaming models do not cover it ([Deepgram](https://developers.deepgram.com/docs/models-languages-overview); [ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/overview/models); [AssemblyAI](https://www.assemblyai.com/docs/speech-to-text/universal-streaming/multilingual-transcription)). In **TTS**, ElevenLabs Flash v2.5 explicitly adds Hungarian (~75 ms claimed, $0.04/1K characters), while Multilingual v2 lacks it. Cartesia Sonic-3.6 lists `hu`. Eleven v4/v4 Turbo claim 90+ languages, but Hungarian is not confirmed on the v4 list itself. No checked open TTS lists it ([ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/overview/models); [Cartesia](https://docs.cartesia.ai/build-with-cartesia/tts-models)).

The tightest constraint is **turn detection**. Deepgram Flux's model-native end-of-turn does not support Hungarian. LiveKit's documented 14-language set does not include it. Whether Hungarian is among Smart Turn's 23 languages was not verified ([Deepgram](https://developers.deepgram.com/docs/models-languages-overview); [LiveKit](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/logic/turns/turn-detector); [smart-turn](https://github.com/pipecat-ai/smart-turn)). Hypothesis, untested: audio-native detectors that read prosody should transfer to an unsupported language better than text-based ones, which would make Smart Turn plus Silero VAD the first thing to try. The fallback is a tuned VAD silence timer, at a latency cost. Among **platforms**, Bland documents `hu` in its call API. Vapi and Retell make language support depend on the chosen STT and TTS, and Retell calls its dashboard "the source of truth." ElevenLabs Agents probably supports Hungarian through its own Flash v2.5 but does not document it for the Agents product ([Bland](https://docs.bland.ai/api-v1/post/calls); [Retell](https://docs.retellai.com/agent/language.md); [Vapi](https://docs.vapi.ai/customization/multilingual)). Across every source, this research found **no Hungarian quality evidence at all**: no WER on 8 kHz phone audio and no MOS for Hungarian voices. Both major independent STT benchmarks are English-centric, and nobody has measured how many characters a minute of spoken Hungarian takes for TTS cost purposes.

The default Hungarian design follows (derived). Build a **cascade**: Deepgram Nova-3 `hu` or ElevenLabs Scribe v2 Realtime for STT, a text LLM, and ElevenLabs Flash v2.5 or Cartesia Sonic-3.6 for TTS, at about $0.021–0.0225/min for the speech layer. Use Silero VAD plus Smart Turn, validated on Hungarian, or a tuned silence timer for endpointing. Prefer Pipecat (BSD-2 throughout, including the turn model) over a LiveKit setup whose bundled detector lacks Hungarian. LiveKit still works through its `vad` or `stt` turn modes ([LiveKit Turns](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/logic/turns/)). Gemini 3.8 Live is the native STS candidate if latency and prosody matter more than voice choice, provided reconnect and resumption are built in. Before launch, build a Hungarian evaluation set from real 8 kHz call audio covering numbers, names, addresses, IBAN-style identifiers and short confirmations ("igen", "nem"). Run it against every candidate STT and turn model, and re-run it whenever a vendor changes a model. On the compliance side, the Hungarian deployment layers the AI Act's disclosure at first interaction on top of the Fgytv. recording, human-first menu and 5-minute answer rules, and Grt. consent for outbound marketing.

## Conclusion

The 2026 shift is not that native speech-to-speech replaced the cascade. It is that the leading STS vendor shipped a cascade-shaped hybrid next to its flagship, a duplex voice layer delegating to a text model, and its own benchmark shows the single-model path completing tasks about half as often. A skill pack should therefore teach the cascade as the reference architecture and STS as a node-level option, and teach turn-taking, not model choice, as the craft. Turn-taking is where the budget goes, where the error pair (false yields against swallowed interruptions) lives, and where language support runs out first. For Hungarian specifically, the binding constraint is the end-of-turn model, not STT or TTS, and no public benchmark will answer that question for you.

The second lesson is economic and legal at once. Speech is now cheap enough (about two cents a minute) that the build-versus-buy decision turns on a single fee, and on whether a platform will sell you EU residency, HIPAA or retention without a sales contract. Meanwhile the law has hardened faster than the vendor docs. In the EU, AI disclosure at the first interaction has been an enforceable obligation since August 2026. US outbound AI calls carry full robocall liability. Hungary prescribes the IVR menu order itself. The patterns that last are the ones that make compliance and quality mechanical: a pre-rendered disclosure greeting, deterministic opt-out outside the model, history truncated to what the caller heard, deterministic normalization before TTS, and an audio regression set in the deployment language that runs whenever a vendor changes something underneath you.
